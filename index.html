    Second languages

    1931 was the first Indian census to ask for a subsidiary language: any language habitually spoken besides the mother tongue. Table XV Part II summarises the answers for 54 mother tongues. These are not complete counts. Returns were compiled only “in those areas in which concurrent vernaculars were found to exist”, and only from the provinces named for each language. In 35 of the 54 entries the printed breakdown also fails to add up to the printed total. The site shows both figures and marks the difference.         The 1931 census and its language return

        The census of 1931 was the last in British India to be fully tabulated. It covered the provinces under British administration and the princely states and agencies, and it included Burma, which was separated from India in 1937. Enumeration took place on the night of 26 February 1931. Table I puts the population at 352,837,778. Table XV, like the other tables built from individual returns, covers 350,529,557 people. The missing 2,308,221 were estimated, not enumerated: 2,212,837 in the agencies and tribal areas of the North-West Frontier; 75,735 in Ahmedabad, where a Congress boycott disrupted the count; and 19,649 in East Manglun in the Shan States. The tables were published in 1933 in Volume I, Part II (Imperial Tables), under J. H. Hutton as Census Commissioner.

        Every household schedule had a mother-tongue column. For the first time there was also a column for any other language habitually spoken “in daily or domestic life”. Infants and deaf-mutes were recorded with their mother’s language. Part I of Table XV, mapped here, gives the mother-tongue figures. Part II gives the subsidiary-language figures, but it is a sample, not a count (see Cautions). Hutton’s report counts 223 languages under Grierson’s classification, against 222 in 1921 (para. 150). Some later summaries give 225.

        Hutton’s Chapter X explains why the census asked about languages beyond pure linguistics. The first reason was the “not unreasonable desire of many Indians for a redistribution of provinces on a linguistic basis”. The second was that the franchise might be extended to large tribal populations. The third was the wish to start primary education “in the real language of the child” (para. 147). The Orissa Boundary Committee asked for the returns from the Oriya–Telugu borderland before they had even been extracted. Orissa became a separate province in 1936, and those figures were among its foundations. The same questions came back after independence, in the States Reorganisation Commission of 1953–55, and 1931 was the last full set of language figures from before Partition.

        The classification (Family › Sub-family › Branch › Group › Language) follows Sir George Grierson’s Linguistic Survey of India, whose introductory volume came out in 1927. On this site you can map any level of it, from “Dravidian Family” down to single entries such as “Kurukh or Oraon”. Where the printed table gives no total for a group, the site adds up its members and says so.

        This site was prompted by India State Stories’ essay “Legacies of the 1931 Linguistic Census”, which discusses these tables and their later use.

        Cautions: read these before trusting a number

        1. “Western Hindi”, “Eastern Hindi” and “Bihari” are a clerk’s division, not what people said. Nobody told an enumerator they spoke “Western Hindi”. The census used the single term Hindustani for both Hindi and Urdu as spoken languages. In the United Provinces Hindustani was the only entry permitted, to keep out the Hindi–Urdu controversy. The India table then reassigned Hindustani to Grierson’s languages by locality. In the United Provinces all 48 million Hindustani speakers went under “Western Hindi”, including the Awadhi-speaking east and the Bhojpuri-speaking districts that Grierson placed under Eastern Hindi and Bihari. That is why the United Provinces shows 100% Western Hindi and almost no Eastern Hindi. Hutton says that even for his linguistic map the Eastern/Western line was “taken arbitrarily as the boundary (more or less) of Agra and Oudh”, and that telling Lahnda from Panjabi was “more than the census enumerators could grasp” (para. 152). The sharp edges these languages show on the map are therefore artefacts of this reclassification. The census recorded no split between Urdu and Hindi at all.
        2. Some returns were politically coloured. In the Oriya–Telugu and Oriya–Bengali borderlands there were rival campaigns over the boundaries of a new Orissa province. Hutton says they led people to “plump” for one language and to hide their bilingualism (para. 148). In Bhopal (in the Central India Agency), Urdu was returned “in deliberate disregard of the actual facts” for Hindus as well as Muslims. Elsewhere some Muslims returned Urdu (Hindustani) as their mother tongue and the local vernacular as a second language, “though the precise opposite was the real fact”. In Bastar, some Gonds returned Halbi instead of Gondi because Halbi carried more status (para. 149).
        3. Tribal languages are probably undercounted. Speakers who were bilingual in a regional language were often recorded under that language. For instance, the Central Provinces superintendent, quoted by Hutton, found that of 84,971 Sawaras “not a single one returned the tribal language”. The new subsidiary-language column helped, but only in some places.
        4. Units are administrative, not linguistic. The map shades whole provinces and agencies. Bengal, Madras or Hyderabad each contained several large language areas. For district detail you need the provincial volumes (see Further reading).
        5. Frontier areas were only partly enumerated. Most of the North-West Frontier’s agencies and tribal areas (an estimated 2.2 million people) were not enumerated individually and are not in Table XV. The “NWFP Agencies & Tribal Areas” row covers only 46,451 people, 96% of them men, in effect the garrisons and posts. The map hatches this unit so that its shading is not read as describing the tribes. The census also left out or estimated parts of the Assam hill tracts and the Burmese frontier. The north-eastern tracts beyond the administered border are hatched on the map and have no figures.
        6. Burma and Aden were part of “India” in 1931. Both are counted in the India totals. Burma accounts for most of the Tibeto-Chinese and Karen figures. Aden sits inside Bombay (row 7), and its figures are shown in Bombay’s panel.
        7. Migrants show up everywhere. A few thousand speakers of a northern language in a southern province usually means soldiers, traders, labourers or officials, not a settled community. The “Females/1,000 males” measure helps spot these groups: migrant groups tend to be mostly male.
        8. The boundaries drawn here are approximate. They are good enough to show which unit is which, but they are not survey-accurate. Data explains how they were built.
        9. Transcription. Every figure was copied by hand from the printed scan and checked arithmetically (see Data). Where the printed table does not add up, the printed figure has been kept and flagged, not silently corrected. For anything you intend to publish, check the figure against the page image. Every language panel links to its page.

        Where the data comes from

        The figures

        The source is Census of India, 1931. Vol. I – India. Part II – Imperial Tables (Delhi: Manager of Publications, 1933), Table XV, “Language”, Part I, “Mother Tongue”. This site uses the scan on the Internet Archive, india.history.resource.92539, scan leaves 477–514 (printed pages 472–509). The same volume is also held by the Gokhale Institute (GIPE) DSpace and Tamil Digital Library.

        The table has 958 numbered columns: Persons, Males and Females for each language and each group total. It has 44 rows: India, the Provinces and States subtotals, 35 provinces, states and agencies, and sub-rows for Aden, Cochin, Travancore and the other Madras States. People read the whole table from page images, without OCR, and then checked it three ways:

        • persons = males + females in every cell;
        • the provincial and state rows add up to the printed Provinces, States and India totals in every column;
        • the members of each group add up to the group total printed in the table.

        A separate reader then re-read a random sample of 250 cells without seeing the transcription. 247 agreed with it. Re-checking the scan showed that the other 3 were the second reader’s own slips (details in source/verification).

        The map

        The base boundaries are the 1931 province/state layer from W. Tennant’s plague_india repository (LGPL-3.0). Its latitudes have been rescaled to WGS84. That layer merges several census units, so they were separated here using modern district boundaries from geoBoundaries, which run close to the old lines at this scale:

        • Sylhet moved from Bengal to Assam, where it belonged in 1931;
        • Bahawalpur goes to the Punjab States Agency;
        • Khairpur goes to the Bombay States;
        • Kalat, Las Bela and Kharan become the Baluchistan States, and Gwadar, then Muscat’s, is shown as foreign;
        • the Malakand states, the agencies and the tribal belt are separated from the NWFP districts;
        • Kathiawar, Cutch and Palanpur form the Western India States Agency;
        • the Simla Hill States are separated from the Punjab States Agency.

        Some 1931 boundaries match modern districts closely, and for these the modern districts replace plague_india’s hand trace. They are Coorg (Kodagu), the NWFP districts and tribal agencies, Tehri Garhwal and Rampur, Manipur, Tripura and Cooch Behar, the Simla Hill States, Bahawalpur, Sind, and Burma (modern Myanmar). The same goes for the lines that became today’s international borders. The Rann of Kutch (from OpenStreetMap, ODbL) is drawn as salt waste, since Table I counts it in no unit’s area. The north-east frontier tracts, Portuguese and French India, and Muscat’s Gwadar are hatched.

        Every modern state and province then has a list of the 1931 units that could lie inside it. Any other unit found there must be an overshoot of the base map, and goes to the neighbouring unit it belongs with. The list keeps deliberate exceptions for real historical outliers: Madras’s Munagala and Bhadrachalam in Telangana, Tonk’s Sironj in Madhya Pradesh, Khariar in Odisha, Seraikela and Kharsawan in Jharkhand, and Datia and Samthar in Uttar Pradesh. After that, thin strips where two sources’ lines disagree, and leftover fragments, go to the unit they sit against. All shapes are simplified together, so neighbours share one line with no gaps or overlaps. The scripts are tools/build_geo.py, tools/geo_audit.py and tools/area_check.py.

        To check the boundaries, each polygon’s area was compared with the area printed for its unit in Table I. The map total comes within 1.4% of the printed all-India figure (1,808,679 sq. mi), and every unit is within about ±10%, except the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, whose coastlines are modern.
